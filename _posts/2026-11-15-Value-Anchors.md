*This post inspired by Ashe Vasquez-Nunez and Richard Ngo - by their example as by our conversations.  All opinions my own.*

This was going to be a post about 'value-anchors', a concept I was going to introduce, and talk about in the context of memetic immune systems.  But I am bad at writing things which could become LessWrong wiki pages.  So instead it became this.

---

I was inspired to write this post by reflecting on how the (Greco-Roman) Classics have shaped my identity and life.  While writing my master's thesis in physics, I was depressed nearly to the point of catatonia - I remember the whole period as a vague blur of phoning it in when meeting with my advisor and watching Simpsons funniest moment clips in bed.  It didn't strike me at the time, but really the only thing I did during this time was to teach myself Latin.

Why Latin?  Mundane subconscious status-y reasons aside, I recognize in retrospect that it was an attempt to find a different worldview from which to understand myself and my life - some stable notion of goodness which would not change the next time I moved cities.

The Romans had a different set of values from those of today; the moral ideal I had inherited and strove towards was that of the 'scientist' as understood by the median American - obsessed, abstemious, brilliant, disembodied; if loud then never ungentle.  Always visibly withdrawn, celibate, and unthreatening.  From my time in the Latin mines, I learned to take seriously a worldview that valued honour, power, craft, and social distinction.

The thing that to me makes this story fun to share is how indirect this process was - it had nothing to do with anything I read, certainly not in Latin.  Instead, it was driven entirely by the hyperstitioned sense of respect for this alien culture that comes out of spending so much effort to learn their language.  The difference wasn't in what I read - it was in the tacit _respect_ I felt reading them.  I could have read a hundred times as much Roman literature in translation as I have, and it would have been rain-off-the-roof.

I think of this as a process of memetic inoculation.  Especially if one is very high-conscientiousness, you can end up internalizing values that denigrate parts of yourself - prominently in the milieu in which I was raised, desires for things like power, wealth, and especially sex.  I didn't grok the idea that one could think of sex (or even romantic love) as an act of compassion rather than of (selfish) indulgence until I was 27.

That one took more than the Romans and the Greeks to figure out - but they helped just by being far enough away from my own memetic world.  It's not that there exists an N such that reading N paragraphs of Caesar makes one think militarism is based actually; instead, it is the act of simply sitting and seeing that unalloyed desire for glory-at-any-price - you come to understand that this too is a part of you - "[humani nihil a me alienum puto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heauton_Timorumenos#Homo_sum,_humani)".  It is a tired thing to hear someone say Hillary or Donald is a lying weasel, but when it is said of Cicero, a man doubtlessly great, it simply stands - this too is in me.

And you see that the part of you that you had been taught to think of as [a lion](https://dantecomedy.com/welcome/inferno/inferno-canto-1/), taught to chain lest it consume you, is in fact a frail and trembling thing, biting only because it has not been taught to speak.

---

Here, learning Latin gave me a **value-anchor** - if I wanted to think about who I was, I could just simulate Cicero and ask him - much in the same way I can my grandfather.  It is never the case that these simulacra overpower my judgement, but that having them permits me to question things I couldn't have otherwise.  They provide ballast.

I think value-anchoring is especially important today because of memetics - the modern world isn't _just_ memetically optimized to hell - the problem isn't merely the **virulence** of our memes, but also the fact that they are so **totalizing** that it is extremely difficult to hold multiple value-anchors.  Consider how **iconoclastic** are all the major ideologies of our day - I'll trust you can name your own examples.

I think 'The Classics' as value-anchors are extremely valuable because they have lain discarded more than been absorbed in the era of the supermeme - people are less likely to bully you for reading Aristotle than Hayek - and being accretions from the era before recommendation algorithms, they are extremely unvirulent.  You can't build an identity on them because they were never engineered to be that in the way that something like ____ is.

Carl Jung has written extensively about 'individuation' in the context of modern society:

> If his soul does not live nothing can save him from stultification. His life is the soil in which his soul can and must develop. He has only the mystery of his living soul to set against the overwhelming might and brutality of collective convictions.
>
> — Jung, *Mysterium Coniunctionis* (CW 14, ¶198)

It's hard to articulate, but this feels subversive in some way, like when I read it, I feel myself rummaging through my bag of value-anchors to see if I can think of any of them that _permit_ this - "Cicero? eh. Confucius? eh... ah! Emerson, yeah".  Another observation is how _slippery_ this kind of unadorned individualism is: I've made this an Anki card, and every time I see it, I am both moved and surprised at how little it has come to mind.  By contrast, something like the Marxist meme is adapted to be constantly before your eyes - "see that guy? exploited surplus value; those people protesting? not class-conscious - they should be _organizing_" - it has an opinion on _fucking everything_ - and if you haven't done your time as a Reddit communist, you can doubtless fill in your own example.

I think it's cringe to imagine that there is an -ism which is 'the answer' - what are we if not little cultures of memes - but how _anti-memetic_ is individualism!  How many teenagers will be told to read *Leaves of Grass* in a time of psychic turmoil?

---

The last and most prominent value-anchors we can have are, of course, the examples of people before us - I have found, for myself at least, that these must be friends and family - someone you have lunch with.  Though there are many people from whom I have learned and without whom my life would be so painfully the smaller and darker, and this year has been one of great change and growth for me, I will take a less emotional but more poignant example from my recent life.

I've had the pleasure of having a few lunches with an old physicist - the kind of guy who has had lunch with a hundred people with Wikipedia pages.  I've learned a lot of physics - he is of course far more knowledgeable than me, but the thing that so insistently impresses itself upon me is the sheer _aliveness_ of science to him.  Even prior to Claude's 'finishing science' looming on the horizon, I grew up thinking about science like an EA - as a natural process which occurs.  But to him physics, and indeed all science, was something that _people did_ in a way that it is not for me.  The mass of irritating facts that make up my molecular biology flashcards are to him _things people fought for_.  What are to me abstruse 3-page asides in my textbook are to him _achievements_ - true brightnesses of the human project - every bit as much as Maxwell's equations.

I remember this when I think about what I do - when I think that there are fifteen hundred other people researching the same thing as me, when I find out someone already had the cool idea I just thought of in 2003.  The point is not that I am inspired to persevere - this is generic - but that I am inspired to believe in my research when most around me think this puerile because Claude will do it in N months.

In the era where the whole AI safety ecosystem has unselfconsciously turned to maximising memeticity - wherein 'make the normies scared' is a _cause area_ or worse a _research objective_ - I wonder how many people ask themselves if they have the right to read *Leaves of Grass*.

My thanks to those who are teaching me that I don't have to ask.
